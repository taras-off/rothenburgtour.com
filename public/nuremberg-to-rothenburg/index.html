Nuremberg to Rothenburg Day Trip: Trains, Tickets and Timing

Close enough that you get the empty morning and the lit evening on the same day.

Most pages treat Nuremberg as a shorter version of the Munich day trip. It is not. It is a different trip, with a different answer, and the difference turns on one thing most pages leave out: Rothenburg sits inside Nuremberg's own transport network.

That single fact removes the constraint that spoils the trip from Munich. This page explains it, gives the timings, and says what to do with the hours it buys you.

Coming from the other direction? The day trip from Munich is a harder case and has its own page. For what to do once you arrive, start with hour-by-hour plan for one day. For the wider view there is complete guide to Rothenburg.

The arithmetic, before anything else

There is no mainline station in Rothenburg. Every rail route ends the same way: change at Steinach (b Rothenburg) onto a branch line that runs hourly and takes 15 minutes.

From Nuremberg you reach Steinach through Ansbach, so the trip is two changes: Nuremberg to Ansbach, Ansbach to Steinach on the RE80, Steinach to Rothenburg on the RB82.

  • By train. 1h10 in the best case, and 1h20 to 1h50 more commonly. Two changes. Take the regional trains on the Nuremberg–Ansbach leg: they are as quick as the long-distance services anyway, and a VGN ticket is a regional-network ticket — before boarding anything branded IC or ICE, check that your ticket covers it.
  • By car. 105 km, about 70 minutes. Rothenburg is 5 km from exit 108 on the A7.
  • By tour coach. Somebody else drives, and you get three to five free hours in town.

The two rail figures are station to station, and Rothenburg station is about ten minutes' walk from the nearest gate; the car figure is driving time and excludes a similar walk in from the car parks.

So the round trip costs you between two and four hours, against five to seven from Munich. That difference is the whole article: it is what turns a compressed, compromised day into an ordinary one.

The ticket most pages miss

Here is the part that changes the trip.

Rothenburg ob der Tauber is inside the VGN — the Verkehrsverbund Großraum Nürnberg, Nuremberg's regional transport network. It appears in VGN's own fare regulations as a tariff area in its own right. So a Nuremberg traveller is not buying a long-distance ticket at all. They are travelling inside their own network, on a network ticket.

That matters for three reasons, and the third is the one that counts.

It is cheaper. A VGN TagesTicket Plus covering the whole network costs €26.50, against €34 for a Bayern-Ticket. The €34 is the Bayern-Ticket's one-person price; each additional counted traveller adds €10, to €74 for five — who counts and who does not is the next paragraph.

Children barely move either price, which is worth knowing because it is the comparison most people need. On a Bayern-Ticket, children up to 5 travel free without limit, and up to three children aged 6 to 14 travel free as well — none of them counted. So two adults with young children pay €44 on a Bayern-Ticket and €26.50 on the Plus: within that allowance the Plus wins by the same €17.50 whether the children are there or not. A fourth child aged 6 to 14 adds €10 to the Bayern-Ticket and nothing to the Plus, so the gap widens — up to the Plus's six heads, beyond which only the Bayern-Ticket can carry you at all.

The two-adult limit below is a maximum, not a minimum, so one person can buy the Plus too. At the top fare level two single journeys come to €29.76 for the return — €14.88 each way for an adult — so the Plus undercuts them. At a lower fare level two singles may come in under €26.50, so price both. VGN also sells a TagesTicket Solo for one traveller; we could not confirm its current whole-network price, so put all three side by side rather than assuming any of them has already won.

It carries a different shape of party. The TagesTicket Plus covers up to six people, at most two of them aged 18 or over — and children count toward that six. A dog travels free, and you can bring bicycles instead of passengers. The Bayern-Ticket stops at five counted travellers, but its free children are not counted, so it can carry more heads than the Plus: two adults, three children aged 6 to 14 and any number of under-5s all travel on one €44 ticket. What the Plus wins on is price, and what it loses on is adults — at most two, which is the whole of the limit below.

It has no morning restriction. This is the point. The Bayern-Ticket is not valid before 09:00 on weekdays — the rule that makes a Munich day trip arrive at lunchtime. The VGN day ticket has no such rule. It runs to 03:00 the next morning, the same finish the Bayern-Ticket has, and if you buy it on a Saturday it covers Sunday as well.

Put that together with a journey of under two hours and the arithmetic is no longer a trade. Leave Nuremberg at 07:30 and you are inside the walls by 09:30 at the latest — ahead of the crowd, on the cheap ticket, on a weekday, even on the slowest connection quoted above. From Munich that combination does not exist on the cheap ticket at all: there it has to be bought back with an advance fare.

One real limit, and it matters. The TagesTicket Plus allows at most two people aged 18 or over. It is a family ticket: two adults and up to four under-18s. Three adults travelling together cannot use it, and should price a Bayern-Ticket at €54 instead. Check the fare level for your own journey when you buy — the zone count, not the city tariff, is what applies from Nuremberg.

One warning for cyclists: on S-Bahn trains and on regional trains without a multi-purpose compartment, bikes cannot be carried Monday to Friday between 06:00 and 08:00 — which rules out the early weekday start this page otherwise recommends. With a bike you either travel after 08:00, and give up the early arrival the page is built on, or you go at the weekend, when there is no blackout at all.

By train, in detail

The branch line is the fixed point. Steinach to Rothenburg, hourly, 15 minutes, on the RB82. Everything else is about how you reach Steinach.

From Nuremberg, that means Ansbach and then the RE80. Two changes, and the second is the branch line every rail passenger makes.

Look up the last train back before you plan the evening — but unlike the Munich trip, you are unlikely to be caught by it. A 1h20 journey means the evening is genuinely available, which is the second thing this trip has and the Munich one does not.

Build the branch line into your plan. Steinach to Rothenburg is hourly, so a missed connection at Steinach costs a full hour.

For comparison, Würzburg is the simpler journey but the wrong ticket. It is about 1h10 as well, but with a single change at Steinach and on regional trains only, and 70 km by road against Nuremberg's 105. The rail times are much the same; what Würzburg saves you is a connection.

What it costs you is this page's whole argument. Würzburg is not in the VGN — it does not appear anywhere in VGN's fare areas, while Rothenburg does. So a Würzburg start gets you the easier journey and none of the ticket: no €26.50 network day ticket, and so no cheap day ticket that is valid before 09:00 on a weekday. You can still leave Würzburg at dawn — you just pay full fare to do it. On the train Würzburg is the simpler trip. On the ticket, Nuremberg is the cheaper one, and that is the difference this page is about.

One exception, and in Germany it is a large one. A Deutschland-Ticket covers the regional legs from either city and has no morning rule either, so for its holder the ticket difference disappears and Würzburg's single change makes it the better base.

It is personal and non-transferable, though, so that only holds if everyone travelling has one. Two adults who each subscribe are both covered; a family with one between them still needs tickets for everybody else, and from Nuremberg the €26.50 Plus covers all of them at once. This page is written for the traveller buying a ticket for the day.

By car

105 km, about 70 minutes, with the old town 5 km from exit 108 on the A7.

Parking is straightforward as long as the car stays outside the walls. The P1–P5 car parks cost €1.30 an hour, capped at €8 for the day, and are charged 09:00–18:00, free outside those hours. There is no time limit at all.

Parking inside the walls is not straightforward. Short-stay spaces are €2.00 an hour, charged daily 08:00–20:00, with a two-hour maximum, which is no use for a day visit. You can drive into the old town: there is no general daytime ban. A day visitor simply has no practical reason to.

Two things to know before you set off. From 2.85 m up you cannot enter the old town without a HöhenVignette, and even with one the ceiling is 3.90 m. And your satnav probably will not know the lane network, so aim for a car park rather than an address inside the walls.

At 70 minutes each way the car's advantage over the train is small: on the best connection the train matches it minute for minute, and on the slowest it is forty minutes behind. It also costs less than the parking plus fuel for one person. But a car costs the same whoever is sitting in it, and rail does not scale the way you might expect. One adult or two both travel on the same Plus, at €26.50 — the price does not move at all. At three the Plus runs out of adult places and you are on a Bayern-Ticket at €54. So it is not a gradual squeeze: it is flat, and then a cliff at the third adult, and that is where the car comes back into contention. If you take the train anyway at that point, take it for the drive you avoid, not to save money: the two changes are what that costs you.

The one date that catches drivers

While the Christmas market runs, from 20 November to 23 December, the old town closes to traffic on market weekends — Saturdays 14:00–18:00 and Sundays 11:00–18:00, residents excepted. That shuts the lanes, not the car parks outside the walls, so a car left outside is unaffected.

What does affect you is that those car parks fill by lunchtime on a market Saturday, and a queue builds at the gates. So come before 11:00 or after 16:00.

Organised tours

If you would rather not assemble the journey yourself, compare organised day trips from Munich, Frankfurt and Nuremberg. A tour coach typically gives you three to five free hours in town, between about 10:30 and 16:00.

From Nuremberg that is a worse deal than it is from Munich, for a reason worth stating plainly: the coach's value is that it absorbs a long, awkward journey, and from Nuremberg the journey is neither. You are paying somebody to solve a problem you do not have, and giving up the early morning and the evening to do it.

Take the coach from Nuremberg if you would rather not navigate two changes, or if you want a guide. Do not take it to save time.

What you actually get

This is where the Nuremberg trip parts company with the Munich one.

A day visitor from Munich gets the middle of the day. A tour coach gets 10:30 to 16:00. From Nuremberg, on a ticket with no morning rule and a journey of under two hours, you can have the early morning and the lit evening on the same day — the two hours this site otherwise says you have to book a hotel for.

The morning. At 07:30 the Plönlein is empty; by 10:30 it has a queue. An early departure from Nuremberg puts you well inside that window. That is the thing a Rothenburg day trip can almost never buy — and from Nuremberg it is simply a question of catching an early train, on the cheap ticket, which is the part Würzburg cannot match.

The evening. The day visitors leave around 16:00. In summer the light holds until near the 21:00 sunset; in December the lit hour starts at 16:20, on 11 December, the earliest sunset of the year. Either way a 1h20 journey home means you do not have to choose between the evening and your bed. The winter case is set out in Rothenburg in winter.

So: eight hours in town in December, and longer in summer if there is a late enough connection home — the page's own warning about evening services applies. Both ends of the day included, for €26.50 shared between two adults and the children. Against that, an overnight stay buys you less here than it does from further away — and this is the one page on this site that will say so.

Where to stay sets out the choice between sleeping inside the walls or outside them, if you do want to stay.

Practical notes

VGN fares are the published 2026 prices, in force from 1 January 2026. Parking charges and the height limit are from the town's fee regulation, version of 31 July 2025. Journey times are from a rail guide last updated in 2025 and a driving guide updated in September 2026. Timetables and prices change — check your date before you travel.

Price the VGN ticket against the Bayern-Ticket before you buy. One or two adults, with or without children: the VGN TagesTicket Plus at €26.50, or the TagesTicket Solo if you are alone and it prices lower. Three or more adults going by rail: a Bayern-Ticket at €54 for three, since the Plus is capped at two adults — though by three the car is back in contention. Children up to 5 travel free on the Bayern-Ticket without limit, and up to three aged 6 to 14 free as well — none of them counted against the five.

Check the fare level for your journey. Nuremberg to Rothenburg is charged by zones crossed, not by the Rothenburg city tariff.

Leave early rather than late. The whole advantage of this trip over the Munich one is the morning, and nothing about the ticket stops you taking it.

Build the branch line into your plan. Steinach to Rothenburg is hourly, so a missed connection at Steinach costs a full hour.

Cash. The Town Hall Tower takes cash only, and so do several smaller places.

Shoes. The whole old town is medieval cobble, and the wall walkway has uneven boards.

In short

Nuremberg to Rothenburg is 1h10 to 1h50 by train or 70 minutes by car, and the right ticket is a VGN day ticket rather than a Bayern-Ticket — cheaper, good for a family of six, and with no 09:00 weekday rule to cost you the morning.

That makes Nuremberg a base from which a Rothenburg day trip reaches both the empty early morning and the lit evening — the easier journey is from Würzburg, but the cheap ticket is only from here. It is not a compromise version of an overnight stay. It is most of one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you get from Nuremberg to Rothenburg ob der Tauber?

By train it is two changes — Nuremberg to Ansbach, the RE80 to Steinach, then the RB82 fifteen minutes to Rothenburg — taking 1h10 at best and 1h20 to 1h50 more commonly. By car it is 105 km and about 70 minutes, with the town 5 km from exit 108 on the A7.

How long does the train from Nuremberg to Rothenburg take?

1h10 in the best case, more often 1h20 to 1h50 depending on the connections. The Steinach to Rothenburg branch line runs hourly, so a missed connection there costs a full hour.

What is the cheapest ticket from Nuremberg to Rothenburg?

Rothenburg is inside the VGN network, so a VGN day ticket applies rather than a Bayern-Ticket. The TagesTicket Plus for the whole network costs €26.50 and covers up to six people, at most two of them aged 18 or over — and since that is a maximum, a single traveller can use it. VGN also sells a TagesTicket Solo for one person; check its price against the Plus. A single adult journey at the top fare level is €14.88. A Bayern-Ticket is €34 for one person and €10 for each traveller after that, with up to three children aged 6 to 14 free, so it only pays from three adults upwards.

Is the Bayern-Ticket valid from Nuremberg to Rothenburg?

Yes, but for one or two adults it is usually the wrong choice. It is €34 for one person and €74 for five, it carries five rather than six, and on weekdays it is not valid before 09:00 — which costs you the quiet early morning that is the main reason to do this trip from Nuremberg. For three or more adults it becomes the right answer, because the VGN family ticket is capped at two.

Can you do Rothenburg as a day trip from Nuremberg?

Yes, and the figures are kinder than from most bases. A journey of under two hours each way, on a ticket with no morning restriction, leaves eight hours or more in town and reaches both the quiet morning before 10:30 and the evening after the day visitors leave.

Is Nuremberg or Munich better for a Rothenburg day trip?

Nuremberg, on the figures. From Munich the journey is 2h30 to 3h30 and the cheap ticket is not valid before 09:00, so you arrive around 12:30 and miss both quiet ends of the day. From Nuremberg neither constraint applies. Würzburg is nearer by road and needs only one change, but it is outside the VGN, so the cheap network ticket this page is built on does not apply from there.

How many hours do you need in Rothenburg?

Four hours covers the wall, the main square and one museum. Six to eight makes it a comfortable day, which a Nuremberg day trip does deliver. What matters more than the total is which hours they are: before 10:30 and after 16:00 are the quiet ones.
